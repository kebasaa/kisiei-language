# Denise Paulme's texts

The ethnologist Denise Paulme recorded these eleven texts in the Kissi country, around Kissidougou and Guéckédou, during two stays with André Schaeffner (October 1945 – June 1946, October 1948 – May 1949). They close her *Documents sur la langue kissi : lexique et textes* [[(Paulme1964, p. 131-165)]]: scenes of farming and cooking, prayers to the ancestors, curses, an account of a funeral and a tale. They are the oldest continuous texts in Northern Kissi available to us.

The [original document](https://github.com/kebasaa/kisiei-language/blob/main/archive/Denise%20Paulme%20-%20Documents%20sur%20la%20langue%20kissi.pdf) and its [full transcription](https://github.com/kebasaa/kisiei-language/blob/main/archive/Denise%20Paulme%20-%20Documents%20sur%20la%20langue%20kissi.md) are in the [archive](../sources/archive.md).

## How to read these texts

Paulme wrote Kisiei in her own phonetic notation. Each line of her text is given here as a small table:

- **at the top**, in small type, the word as Paulme wrote it;
- **in the middle**, the same word **respelled in the Bible's orthography**, underlined according to its status (see [How to read this guide](../introduction/how-to-read.md)): words the Bible does not contain are marked "respelled";
- **at the bottom**, Paulme's word-by-word gloss, translated from her French.

Her free translation follows each text (translated from her French), then her notes. The respelling follows the rules on the page [From the old to the new spelling](../orthography/old-and-new.md#older-northern-sources-paulme-1964-and-sandara-kissia); where the Bible uses a word differently from Paulme, the Bible's usage decides the spelling and grammar of this guide.

<p class="ks-free" markdown="1">The first enemy is the bullfrogs. They dig up the rice; they come out at night, and they cannot be caught. The weaver birds eat the rice during the day. The children watch over the birds. The mice cut /the stalks/ of rice that are starting to come up /out of the ground/. I fenced the field with reeds, I dug a pit /into which/ the field rats /will fall/. My mother weeded the field; the women weeded the rice in the field. The names of these weeds are *sandyesayo* and *yawa*. The enemies of upland rice /2/ are the monkeys, the mice, the field rats and the *yawa* weeds. 2. Up to this point the informant has been speaking of the lowland rice fields.

<p class="ks-free" markdown="1">After the harvest, the husband gives his wife the rice. /Ten or twenty bundles. This is for eating. It is put in large baskets under the veranda. One large basket will hold about ten loads.</p>

<p class="ks-free" markdown="1">First, the rice is threshed in the field. The threshed rice is put in sacks that are carried to the village, where the bundles are put in large baskets. A well-filled basket is enough to feed two people for the whole dry season/.</p>

<p class="ks-free" markdown="1">The woman takes a winnowing tray and winnows for a long time, then puts [the rice] in a large pot with plenty of water, lights the fire and brings it to the boil. When the rice has boiled, she takes it out of the water. She spreads the rice on banana leaves on the ground to dry it.</p>

<p class="ks-free" markdown="1">The next day, early in the morning, the boiled rice is put back in the pot with just enough water to cover it. The rice absorbs the steam. The woman takes it off the fire and spreads it out to cool, in the shade, in the hut or under the veranda. Once it is cool, she carries it into the sun. Then several women or girls get together to pound the rice.</p>

<p class="ks-free" markdown="1">When the rice has been pounded, in the evening the woman lights a fire and puts water in the pot, which she lets boil; she takes some rice /on average, two measures for four people/, washes it well, pours it into the boiling water, lets it boil for a long time, and draws off the excess water, which she gives to her husband, or keeps for herself if she is very greedy. To the dried rice she adds salt, chillies and soumbara in a pot, which she covers. When everything is cooked, she takes the pot off the fire. She pours [the rice] into a calabash for her husband, into another calabash for herself and the girls, and into yet another calabash for the visiting boys. She gives [his] to her husband. They eat at the same time, but separately.</p>

<p class="ks-free" markdown="1">/A very free translation, much fuller than the original text. It seemed better to keep the explanations, which show the informant's enthusiasm for a subject dear to him/. *(Translated from Paulme's French.)*</p>

<p class="ks-free" markdown="1">On Saturday they brought Kaba's body. He had been ill for a long time. He refused to go to hospital; he had gone to Firana, to his mother's family. Paulme's notes on the translation:

1. This task consists in cutting the grass of the lowland so that it rots where it lies, giving a first manure.
2. *tyawa*, to turn over the soil.

Paulme's notes on the interlinear:

1. *i tyi tya*: I have finished the *tya* – *Bara ityɛy ɔ tyu*: the field work is finished.
2. *i san malo*: I sow the rice – *i sanni malo sodi*: I will sow the rice tomorrow.
3. *i tɔa yuwa*: I watch over the birds – *i tyɔ yuwa tuo*: I am watching over the birds – *mi tɔani yuwa* = I will watch over the birds.
4. *mi kwau malo*: I have cut the rice – One also says: *mi tyi malo n kuɔ*: I have finished cutting the rice – *kwau* = *kuɔ* – comes from *muɔy*, knife.

<p class="ks-free" markdown="1">The porcupine and the hare set off to travel. The hare's name was "Stranger", the porcupine's name was "Porcupine". They left. Night fell; they arrive in a village and ask for the chief. The chief asks their names: "My name is Porcupine." – "My name is Stranger." They are given a hut. A girl brings them rice: the rice is for the strangers. The porcupine wanted to eat, but Stranger said: "This rice is for me." The hare ate all the rice.</p>

<p class="ks-free" markdown="1">They sleep. The porcupine does not sleep: he gets up, takes Stranger's clothes and goes off to the fields. He pulls up cassava, sweet potatoes and groundnuts, and soils Stranger's clothes. Stranger sleeps on; the porcupine goes back to sleep. In the morning the people of the village see that things have been stolen from the field. The chief questions the strangers; he sees Stranger's clothes, soiled. The strangers come out. The porcupine says: "I was asleep. Ask Stranger about his clothes: they are soiled, look. He is the thief." The chief asks the porcupine: "How many blows?" – "That is not much: strike him a hundred blows, you strikers. Call the diviner, and we two will drink the medicine." The diviner comes. Stranger drinks first: he vomits the rice. The porcupine drinks: he vomits the stolen things, sweet potatoes, cassava, groundnuts. The chief asks Stranger: "How many blows?" – "That is not much: three hundred." They beat the porcupine. The chief said: "One must not travel with Stranger."</p>

<p class="ks-free" markdown="1">They set off into the bush. The porcupine says: "I am tired, I am going to wash." At the stream he sees some hunters and tells them: "My boy, you will see him, he has bellows." The hunters find Stranger. Stranger says: "These are not bellows, these are ears!" The hunters pull off his ears. The hare is angry. They continue the journey and meet some fishermen. Stranger asks them: "Where are you going?" – "We are going fishing." – "My boy is behind, he has arrows." The fishermen saw the porcupine: "Stranger has given us the arrows." – "These are not arrows, they are quills, my quills!" They killed the porcupine and pulled out his many quills. Stranger goes home. He said: "I have a cunning heart: I have no ears any more, but the porcupine is dead." Whoever travels with someone more [cunning] than himself does not have a wise heart. *(The guide's translation, from Paulme's word glosses; the typescript gives no running translation. Square brackets mark an added word.)*</p>

??? note "Paulme's notes (pp. 161–165)"
    1. *wa* for *a*, *na*, third person plural pronoun.
    2. *kerane*, *kerani*: they will travel. The future is rendered by the habitual /suffix *a*/ and *ni*, which expresses intention, possession, belonging – /*tyɛ ɔ Fasa ni*: Fasa's hut, for Fasa/.
    3. *a tyɛn*: they got up, they left, they flew; an idea of movement. *a tyɛn... ni*: they intended to; when an infinitive is the direct object of a verb, the suffix *ni* is added to it.
    4. *sando dolã*: in the genitive construction, the name of the possessor precedes that of the thing possessed. *do*: for pronouns: *fiŋa* /1/ *nɔm* /2/ *dolã* /3/: /what is the/ name /3/ of your /2/ father /1/.
    5. *sando dolã mallo*: the verb "to be" is often understood.
    6. *a kwɛ*: they have left. The suffix *ɛ* is the usual mark of the perfect.
    7. *natul*: root *tul* /= go down/, *tulɔ*, descent.
    8. *a tiya*: the present does not differ from the habitual; suffix *a*.
    9. *ɔ*: particle indicating movement, intention; used with *ni*, it indicates belonging – *ɔ bundo ni*: in the forest; *tyɛy Fasa ni*: the hut of /for/ Fasa.
    10. *ya dolã*, my name. *Ya*, first person singular pronoun; used as a possessive, it precedes the noun.
    11. *ya mallo*, for *ya /dolã/ mallo*, my name is stranger.
    12. *ma yɔŋu da*: they are given. "One" is rendered by the third person plural + the active – the same form often expresses a passive.
    13. *tyɛyo*: a hut; *tyɛ*, *tyɛy*, hut. *o*, indefinite article, suffixed to many nouns ending in a vowel, gives a diphthong.
    14. *tyu*: brings – exact tense?
    15. *malla*, plural of *mallo*, the stranger; *ne*, *ni*, preposition.
    16. *wa le*, was for /wanted/; *wa*, perfect of the verb "to be"; *le*, preposition with various senses: with, against, because of, why, how.
    17. *duo*, noun: eating, food.
    18. *mi*, definite article; often used to give an emphatic sense /"it is the..."/.
    19. *a*, that; preposition linking main clause + subordinate clause.
    20. *nuni*, for me; *nu*, first person singular object pronoun; *ni*, suffix of belonging.
    21. *dɛ*: the suffix *ɛ* marks the perfect = has finished eating.
    22. *mumu bɔ*: all the rice: *bɔ*, adjective, means big; used as an adverb it translates as all, together; or all, sufficient, enough, much.
    23. *lɔlte*: does not sleep; *te*, *nte*, *re*, *le*, suffix expressing negation: *ɔ tyɔ bɔre*: that is not much.
    24. *mo tyan*, he gets up; present. Compare above, *a tyɛn kɛra ne*: they got up /in the past/ to travel.
    25. *wurɔla*, plural – singular: *wurɔ*.
    26. *ɔ*: particle of movement: towards, into.
    27. *puli*: clay, earth, fields, plural – singular: *pulo*. Plurals in *i* are rare.
    28. *mo suŋu*: he has pulled up, perfect.
    29. *tyɔ luã*, continues his sleep; progressive form for *ɔ lɔl*, he sleeps; *luã*, noun, sleep. *ɔ tyɔ*: he is, copula – The progressive can also be rendered by the form *luã ɔ tyo ni*: he is sleeping.
    30. *idiyo*: it is morning – *di*: morning, *diyo*, goodbye.
    31. *wana tyɛya*: the people of the village; *tyɛy*: treated as an adjective, it takes the *a*, plural mark, to agree with the noun it refers to.
    32. *wana tyɛya a tye*: the third person pronoun /*a*/ reinforces the subject noun /*wana tyɛya*/.
    33. *a kimbu nyyɔ̃*; things have been stolen. "One" is rendered by the third person plural /*a*/; *kimbu*, exact tense? – *nyyɔ̃*: things, plural, for *nyɛy*.
    34. *wurola ta nɔryela*: *nɔryela*: plural.
    35. *mi*: emphatic for *i*, first person singular pronoun.
    36. *ũi/y/o*: the thief; *o*, suffixed article /= third person singular pronoun/.
    37. *vɛna... nɛ*, how many /*nɛ*, interrogative particle/.
    38. *ɔ tyɔ bɔre*: that is not much; *ɔ*, third person singular pronoun; *tyɔ*, copula /"is"/; *bɔ*, adjective, big, can be used as an adverb meaning enough, sufficient, much; *re*, negative particle.
    39. *lɔnya luaya* /*lɔyɛ* and *luaya*/.
    40. *min*: inclusive first person plural pronoun.
    41. *kɔwã*, literally blood; by extension, any magical preparation containing blood.
    42. *kul*, *kɔlu*.
    43. *kwandye*, stolen things; from *ɔ kwandu*: he has stolen, taken.
    44. *la lɔ̃*: strike /imperative/. *ma lua*: they struck.
    45. *la kɛrania*: one must not travel.
    46. *mallole*, with the stranger. *le* has the general sense of against, because of, that is why, how, because; suffixed, it takes the sense of: with.
    47. *ɔ...ni*: in the...; indicates location: *ɔ tala ni*: in the calabash; and also, for persons, belonging – *tyɛy ɔ Fasa ni*, Fasa's hut.
    48. *purando*: action noun /suffix *ndo*/ for *i pura*: I wash, or I wash myself.
    49. *bwɛy*, for "boy", servant /English word/.
    50. *nɔnu*, for me; compare *nuni*, note 20. The vowels change according to the context.
    51. *tyani*: future /*i tyɛ* = I saw; *i tya* = I see; *i tyani*, I will see/.
    52. Forge bellows, of the pot type, covered with skin /*kurã*: skin bag/.
    53. *ɔ tyɔra*: it is not /*ra*, negation/. — 53 b. *kure* for *kuran re*.
    54. *nira nira ni*: repetition often has an emphatic role.
    55. *ma atiya wana yɔya*: is *atiya* the verb *ɔ tyan*, he gets up, he has left, which with a direct object would take the sense "to meet"?
    56. *wana yɔya*: *yɔya*? fishing.
    57. *yuna*: asks.
    58. *nya*, you (plural), for *la* or *da*.
    59. *nɛ*: interrogative particle.
    60. *n'tyɔre*: we are going? *i tyɔ yɔya*.
    61. *kɔli*: adverb of place: behind, late /from *kɔ*, back/.
    62. *yɔŋu*: has given.
    63. *siyã siyã nyani*: same form as above, *nirã nirã ni*, note 54.
    64. *'andulo*: at his home. *ndu* = third person singular pronoun. *a*, *ɔ*, particle of movement, meaning towards, against, in front of; not often used alone, but in expressions such as *ɔ...ni*, in; *ɔ...tyo*, on top.
    65. *i nɔre la*: is this a double negation? /*i nɔ* = I have/ – *i nɔre*: I do not have, *i nɔrela*.
    66. *nɛu nɔm kɔllo tyundo re*: *u nɔm*: more than you.
