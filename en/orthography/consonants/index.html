    Consonants¶

    Simple consonants¶

    Letter Pronunciation Example Meaning
    b as in bed bala hands
    c [c], between t and ch, like t-y in got you said quickly cuaa child
    d as in day diolan name
    f as in fun fula go out
    h always pronounced, as in English house hun come
    k as in skin, without a puff of air kalaa mother
    l as in let; ll is a long l, as in tall lamp lɛŋ / kɔllo ground / heart
    m as in man mɛnndan water
    n as in net nin in
    ŋ ng of English sing ŋuɛɛnu five
    ɲ ny of canyon ɲɛ thing
    p as in spin, without a puff of air pee river
    s always hissing, even between two vowels sɔɔn words
    t as in stop, without a puff of air tiɔn two
    v as in van vana person
    w w of watt, only after a vowel taw much, many
    y y of yes yáá three

    In Southern Kissi, b and d are implosive and occur mainly at the beginning of words (Childs 1995, p. 27); c there varies between [tʃ] and an almost velar articulation (Childs 1995, p. 31).

    w and y are classed as semivowels (Keita 1979, p. 14): y begins syllables (yɔmndo “tree”), whereas w only ends them (nawvo “cow” is divided naw-vo). In Southern Kissi, [w] also begins syllables and is pronounced [v] before i, e and often a (Childs 1995, p. 33-36); the Bible then writes v: vana “person” corresponds to Southern Kissi wànà (Childs 1995, p. 199).

    The sounds c, ŋ, ɲ¶

    Rule

    Sound Write No longer write (1979)
    [c] c: cuaa, cɛiyo ty: tyuaa, tyièiyo
    [ŋ] ŋ: ŋuɛɛnu, naŋ nh: nhuèènu
    [ɲ] ɲ: ɲɛ, ɲumndan ny: nyè, nyumndan

    The sequences ny and nh are now written only when n and y (or h) belong to two different syllables, which is rare (Bɛnyamɛn “Benjamin”).

    Labial-velar consonants: kp, gb¶

    kp and gb are pronounced by closing the back of the mouth (as for k, g) and the lips (as for p, b) at the same time. They are single consonants, not sequences k + p.

    • kp occurs at the beginning and in the middle of words: kpemaa “covenant”, kpow “all”, kpede “all together”.
    • gb occurs only in the middle of words, after a nasal vowel (Keita 1979, p. 15): langbanɔɔ “adult man”, kpingbi. In the Bible, gb always follows n, m or ŋ. In Southern Kissi, [gb] is described as the form kp takes after a nasal (Childs 1995, p. 28, 31).

    Prenasalised consonants: mb, nd, ng¶

    These consonants begin with a short nasal resonance.

    • mb: pɔmbɔɔ, kumbu, pembei.
    • nd: very frequent, especially in pronouns and class suffixes: ndu “him”, nda “them”, yɔmndo, mɛnndan.
    • ng: tenga “shine”, kpengii “side, direction”.

    The correspondence sheet

    The “new spelling” sheet in the archive Nouvelle orthographe kissi (tableau de correspondances) (s.d.) says “mb = nb”. The Bible never writes nb and keeps mb (pɔmbɔɔ, tɛɛmbuu): write mb.

    n and ŋ¶

    At the end of a stem, Kisiei distinguishes n and ŋ: nan “pull” / naŋ “good”. The old spelling wrote both as n; the Bible (BKG) writes ŋ where [ŋ] is pronounced:

    • at the end of a word: naŋ, lɛŋ, baŋ, yɔŋ;
    • before a suffix that begins with n: the stem keeps its ŋ, and the suffix is added after it. So write ŋn where 1979 wrote nn:
    Stem Full form (Bible) Old spelling
    lɛŋ lɛŋnden “country” lènnden
    yɔŋ yɔŋnun “become” yönnun
    — ciŋnde “tooth” tyinnde
    — tɔŋndo “word, command” tönndo

    On the other hand, when the stem ends in n, write nn: mɛnndan “water” (stem mɛn-), vannda “people” (stem van-).

    In Southern Kissi, n never occurs at the end of a syllable: the final nasal there is always [ŋ] (or m), and it becomes n when a suffix beginning with a vowel is added (Childs 1995, p. 22-24). The Bible, which distinguishes final -n and -ŋ (hun “come” / naŋ “good”), remains the reference.

    How to choose

    To know whether to write nn or ŋn, look at the short form of the word: if it ends in ŋ (lɛŋ), the long form is written with ŋn (lɛŋnden). When in doubt, check in the Bible or in the lexicon.

    Before g, simply write ng, never ŋg: tenga, kpengii.

    The long consonant ll¶

    l can be long; it is then written ll. The first l closes a syllable, the second opens the next one (Keita 1979, p. 16): kɔllo kɔl-lo “heart”, hangullo han-gul-lo “heat”, bolle bol-le “head, top”. ll appears mainly when a suffix beginning with l is added to a stem ending in l: vallo “(the) work”, dimullo “say to”. In Southern Kissi, a suffix beginning with a vowel likewise takes an l after a stem in l (Childs 1995, p. 151). Before a suffix in l, the second l there often becomes t (Childs 1995, p. 155-156); the Bible also has this lt in some words (hɔlten “eye”, bóltan “heads”), but not in others (bollen “head”).

    The apostrophe: n'¶

    Before a consonant, an n that forms a syllable on its own is written n' and attached to the following word: n'van tɔ́ “ten people” Gen 42:3, n'rɛsɛnndo “vine”. This is a nasal prefix; its meaning is described in the Grammar chapter.
