    Word division and the hyphen¶

    Knowing where a word begins and ends is one of the main difficulties in writing Kisiei. This chapter brings together the rules followed by the Bible (BKG).

    Full form and stem form of the noun¶

    A noun has two written forms (see Noun classes):

    • the full form, with its class suffix, when the noun stands alone or at the end of a phrase: cɛiyo “the house”, yɔmndo “the tree”, diolan “the name”;
    • the stem form, without a suffix, when the noun is followed by a determiner, an adjective, a number or a possessor: cɛi kon “that house”, yɔm kon “that tree”, diola ndɔlan “his/her name”.

    Rule

    The stem form is a separate word, separated by a space from what follows. Do not write cɛikon or cɛi-kon.

    Southern Kissi has the same alternation: the noun's suffix moves onto the word that modifies it (Childs 1995, p. 150-151).

    Grammatical words are written separately¶

    Pronouns, auxiliaries, prepositions, demonstratives and possessives are written as separate words:

    O co kɛndia le “he (she) is not well” 1 Sam 19:14 — o “he, she”, co “be”, kɛndia “in good health”, le (negation).

    Mí Hala ca maa ɲɛ-tengaa naŋ “And God saw that the light was good” Gen 1:4.

    The final negation le / te is also a separate word; write te after n, m, ŋ or l, and le after a vowel or w (see Negation; in Southern Kissi, te follows any consonant (Childs 1995, p. 89, 125)): La kei nanu le “do not pass through here” Num 20:20, Vana diinin nɔm te “no one will kill you” 2 Sam 19:24.

    The hyphen¶

    The Bible uses the hyphen a great deal (nearly 18,000 times). It has three uses.

    1. The referent that closes a phrase¶

    When a noun phrase ends with something other than the noun itself — a relative clause, a complement, a proper name — the noun's class is repeated by a referent (a class marker) which is attached by a hyphen to the last word of the phrase:

    Group Referent Example
    class o (singular) -o, -ndo, -yo, -lo kɔngɔ vesɛi fula-o “the dry land that appeared” Gen 1:10
    Kɔl Hala-o “the Spirit of God” Gen 1:2
    o co paalen-ndo “the one that is the sun” Gen 1:16
    class a (plural, persons) -a, -nda visia pɔmbɔa kela lɛŋ-nda “the small animals that walk on the ground” Gen 1:24
    class an -an mɛmma co lebɛngu-an “the waters that are below” Gen 1:7
    class e / i -e, -nde sɔɛi Mɛlɛka dimul ndu-e “the word that the Lord had spoken to him” Gen 15:6

    The choice between -o, -ndo, -yo or -lo (and likewise -a / -nda, -e / -nde) depends on the end of the word to which the referent is attached: after a nasal write -ndo, -nda, -nde (lɛŋnden-ndo, nin-nda); elsewhere the simple form (fula-o, coo-an). The Agreement page gives these forms in detail.

    1979 usage

    In 1979, these referents are presented as separate relative pronouns (vo, ve, van, lan…), written after the verb (Keita 1979, p. 88-89): Domaa n pendu vo sèsa “the shirt that you bought has torn”. The Bible writes the same marker attached by a hyphen at the end of the relative clause; the form vo almost never appears on its own. Follow the Bible. The description of Southern Kissi points the same way: the noun's class ending is a suffix placed at the end of the relative clause (Childs 1995, p. 285-286).

    2. Compound words¶

    The hyphen joins the elements of a compound noun whose parts keep their form (in Southern Kissi, these non-fixed compounds are pronounced as separate words (Childs 1995, p. 216)): Cɛi-Hala-o “the house of God” Gen 28:17, fuɲa-mamaa “ancestor”, fonda-loamaa-lasálàá “altar (place where sacrifices are burnt)”, cɛi-kola “tent (house of cloth)”.

    Very common compounds are often written as a single word: vanapɔnɔɔ “man”, vanalanɔɔ “woman”, halataalaa “sky”. See Compound nouns.

    3. Reduplicated words¶

    A word repeated to express intensity, distribution or repetition is written with a hyphen: taw-taw “very much indeed”, ɲɛ-ɲɛ “anything at all”, yɛ́-yɛ́ “each kind”, bɛndu-bɛnduan “very big (ones)”.

    The prefix n'¶

    The nasal prefix n' is attached by an apostrophe to the following word: n'van tɔ́ “ten people”. See Consonants.

    Summary¶

    Case Write Example
    noun alone full form, one word cɛiyo
    noun + determiner stem form + space cɛi kon
    phrase closed by a referent hyphen fula-o
    compound hyphen (or a single word if lexicalised) fuɲa-mamaa
    reduplication hyphen taw-taw
    grammatical words separate words O co kɛndia le
