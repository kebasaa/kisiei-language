    Vowels¶

    The seven vowels¶

    Kisiei has seven vowels, like Southern Kissi (Childs 1995, p. 35). Two of them are written with letters that are not part of the English alphabet.

    Letter Pronunciation Example Meaning
    i i of machine pilɛ one
    e close e, like ay in say but without the glide towards ee pelei foot
    ɛ open e, as in bed lɛŋ ground, earth
    a a of father bala hands
    ɔ open o, as in law (British thought) pɔmbɔɔ small, younger
    o close o, like o in go but without the glide towards oo yondoo bush
    u oo of food cuaa child

    English speakers should keep every vowel pure: Kisiei e and o do not glide like English say and go.

    Rule

    Always distinguish e / ɛ and o / ɔ: they are different vowels, which change the meaning of words. Do not write é, è or ö: the è and ö of the old spelling become ɛ and ɔ (see From the old to the new spelling).

    Long vowels¶

    A long vowel is pronounced for longer than a short vowel; it is written by doubling the letter: aa, ee, ɛɛ, ii, oo, ɔɔ, uu. Length changes the meaning of a word (Childs 1995, p. 37-38); it is the most frequent source of errors among learners (Keita 1979, p. 12).

    Short Meaning Long Meaning
    velu beget veelu call
    yema sister yeema want
    bala hands baala work
    koma then, again kooma blood

    Examples from the Bible: Mɛɛ Adam velu Sɛt “when Adam begat Seth” Gen 5:4; Mí Mɛlɛka Hala veelu vanapɔnɔɔ “The Lord God called the man” Gen 3:9.

    Final long vowel and the full form¶

    Very often, the long vowel at the end of a word is not part of the stem: it is the class suffix of a noun or the infinitive marker of a verb. It disappears when the word takes its stem form:

    Stem form Full form
    kala ndɔ “his/her mother” kalaa “the mother”
    fula “go out (he goes out)” fulaa “to go out, the going out”
    dio “eat (he eats)” dioo “to eat, the eating”

    So do not “correct” kala ndɔ to kalaa ndɔ: the short form is the right one before a determiner (see Word division and Noun classes).

    Vowel sequences¶

    Two different vowels can follow each other; they are written as they are heard, without a diaeresis or apostrophe:

    • ia, io, iɔ, iɛ: fuɲaa, dioo, tiɔn, ciɛɔn;
    • ua, uɛ, ui: cuaa, ŋuɛɛnu, kuí;
    • ɛi, ei, oi: cɛiyo, pelei, hoeiye.

    These sequences are described in 1979 as diphthongs and triphthongs (Keita 1979, p. 11). In Southern Kissi, i and u before another vowel are pronounced as the semivowels [j] and [w], while sequences ending in i or u (ei, ɛi, oi, au…) have the length of a long vowel (Childs 1995, p. 39-42). The Bible (BKG) always writes them with the ordinary letters.

    Nasal vowels¶

    When a vowel is followed by n at the end of a syllable, it is pronounced nasalised: an, ɛn, in, on, ɔn, un. No sign is added; simply write the vowel followed by n:

    diolan “name”, mɛnndan “water”, nin “in”, kon “that…”, sɔɔn “words”, hun “come”.

    In Southern Kissi, this final nasal is analysed as a consonant [ŋ] that nasalises the preceding vowel; it would continue earlier nasal vowels (Childs 1995, p. 88-89).

    Note

    At the end of a stem, the nasal sound can be [n] or [ŋ], and the Bible distinguishes them: nan “pull” (má nan Yosɛf “they pulled Joseph” Gen 37:28) is not naŋ “good”. See Consonants.

    The sequences am, om, im… are not nasal vowels, but a vowel followed by the consonant m (Keita 1979, p. 13): yɔmndo is divided yɔm-ndo.
